Science is not a set of facts or theories. It is an act, a way of thinking and of doing. My courses are built around this idea, geared to get students to a place where they can think about, and do, science themselves.

Science is also a uniquely human endeavor, inextricable from the prejudices and poetry of human culture. I try not to hide this from students behind claims of “scientific objectivity”.

The best classrooms are laboratories: places of experimentation and exploration. That applies to me as well. My coursework changes. We try new things. Sometimes they work well, sometimes not so well. As a scientist, it can be dangerous to be too afraid of failure.

I have been teaching university courses for more than 15 years. I am currently teaching two: Frontiers of Science and Animal Behavior through Fieldwork. They are completely different, in both their content and their challenges. I love teaching both.

## Frontiers of Science

Frontiers of Science is part of Columbia College’s core curriculum; everyone takes FroSci as a freshman. Serving more than 700 students each semester is a massive undertaking, and we have more than 15 full-time faculty who collaborate to create and run the course. Frontiers is the ultimate teaching challenge, covering everything from special relativity, to the biochemistry of cancer, to the neuroscience of memory formation.

## Animal Behavior through Fieldwork

Animal Behavior is my favorite class to teach. It’s an ideal summer course (which is how I’m teaching it now), and the perfect intro science course, though few departments seem to have noticed. The questions are immediately tangible. The methods are rigorous yet easy to explain. And animals are everywhere, so you’re never short of fieldwork possibilities. My class pushes students out into the world, forces them to form hypotheses, make observations, draw conclusions. The best way to learn science is to be a scientist.
